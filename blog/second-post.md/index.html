Ode to the computers of my past

Aug 13, 2026

I have owned a lot of computers over the years, though still fewer than my dad. He still has 8086 and 286 PCs in the basement. “They might be worth something someday,” he would tell my mom whenever she asked why they were still there.

Early desktops

  1. 386 era box The 386 was less a computer than a first cockpit. I learned to shoot down Focke-Wulf Fw 190s and MiG-19s in Chuck Yeager’s Air Combat on this machine, which is to say I learned that a CRT could become a sky if the pixels were persuasive enough.

  2. 486 upgrade phase The 486 was the first clear step up from the 386, the first machine that made responsiveness feel like a luxury. Especially the turbo button, that tiny promise of more. It played X-Wing well and Chuck Yeager’s Air Combat ran much smoother. That was about the limit of what it ran smoothly, but limits are easier to forgive when they arrive with a button labeled turbo.

  3. TigerDirect special: 486-ish platform upgraded to Pentium MMX The TigerDirect special changed identities multiple times as parts were replaced, a desktop Ship of Theseus held together by optimism and whatever would still fit the board. I think it ran Windows 95 and then 98. Don’t ask me where I got the activation keys.

  4. Custom AMD Athlon 64 5700 build The AMD Athlon 64 5700 build was the first machine I chose on purpose, part by part, a declaration that a computer could be not just owned but composed. It didn’t overclock very well.

  5. Custom AMD Athlon 64 XII 6400 build Then came the Athlon 64 X2 6400, a dual-core CPU and a GeForce 8600GT 512MB in the same case as the build above, with 4GB DDR2 RAM at 800MHz. Not extravagant, exactly, but enough to make the old case feel briefly heroic while playing Oblivion.

  6. Lampstand iMac, 700MHz The 700MHz lampstand iMac was the iconic lamp-arm iMac era machine, and I wish I still had it in my parents’ basement. My first Unix-like machine, and maybe the first computer I owned that seemed designed by someone who believed computers could be furniture.

Laptops and nearby side quests

  1. Dell Inspiron 5000 from University of Iowa Surplus The Dell Inspiron 5000 from University of Iowa Surplus had a 1GHz PIII and a broken lid latch, but it ran Counter-Strike surprisingly well and felt better than some Pentium 4 systems I used around the same time. If I remember right, it likely had somewhere around 512MB of RAM.

  2. The 2007 17-inch MacBook Pro, with a Core 2 Duo T7800 and 4GB RAM, runs Manjaro Linux okay with an SSD and has the 1900x1200 screen. It runs hot!

  3. Acer Atom netbook (circa 2009, Windows) The Acer Atom netbook, circa 2009, was great for taking notes in A&P class. Dark blue, single-core, likely an Atom N2xx-era configuration with 1GB RAM. A modest little slab of compromise, but it fit in a bag and did its job.

  4. 11-inch Dell touchscreen with AMD A6-1450 quad APU The 11-inch Dell touchscreen with an AMD A6-1450 quad APU came with 4GB RAM and an HDD. I upgraded it, but it still felt slow on Windows 8 and later Windows 10. Single-channel memory: the quiet tragedy written into the spec sheet.

  5. 13-inch 2012 unibody MacBook Pro (donated by Brett) The 13-inch 2012 unibody MacBook Pro, donated by Brett, became something more than a hand-me-down. I upgraded it to an SSD and 16GB RAM. This is the machine I started serious development work on, and I used it until 2021 before giving it to friends’ kids. Some machines are not retired so much as passed along. I wonder if it’s still in use.

  6. 2010 27-inch iMac (fully upgraded, donated by Brett) The 2010 27-inch iMac, also donated by Brett, was fully upgraded from i3 to i7 with a Radeon 5700-series 1GB-class GPU. I played Dead by Daylight and Oblivion on Windows, and also used Ubuntu for development with the 2.93GHz Nehalem quad-core. A huge silver window.

  7. M1 Mac mini, 16GB RAM The M1 Mac mini with 16GB RAM is still my current main work machine. Performance is fine, but storage is tight. Every era has its bottleneck.

  8. MSI GF63 10SC with GTX-1650 4GB from Walmart for under $500 US. (2021) The MSI GF63 10SC with GTX-1650 4GB came from Walmart for under $500 US in 2021. Good deal? Maybe. The battery firmware behaved like it had selective memory, including charge-state confusion. Controller reset button underneath: never a confidence booster.

  9. 2015 MacBook Pro pair: 13-inch and 15-inch The 2015 MacBook Pro pair, 13-inch and 15-inch, feel like late Intel MacBooks trying to outlast their own category. I am typing on the 15-inch now, running macOS 13 with OCLP. Linux works well on the 13-inch overall, but on the 15-inch Southern Islands AMD graphics are tricky: amdgpu issues, Radeon without Vulkan support, and Electron/Chromium often needing OpenGL paths.

  10. ThinkPad E431 (upgraded to quad-core i7 3740QM 2.7GHz) The ThinkPad E431 was upgraded to a quad-core i7 3740QM at 2.7GHz. I added a small boot SSD, likely mSATA or M.2 with SATA II speeds, and a 1TB SSD used for NAS storage duties. A laptop gradually becoming infrastructure is one of the purest forms of reuse.

  11. HP ProBook G2 (broken screen, now Proxmox) The HP ProBook G2, broken screen and all, became a headless Proxmox machine with the same small SSD as the E431 for boot and an HDD for storage. A machine without a face, still doing work.

  12. Surface Pro 7+ curb find with a 15-inch portable display The Surface Pro 7+ was a curb find paired with a 15-inch portable display. It runs Fedora with linux-surface. The setup works well, but Fedora’s release timing does not always line up cleanly with linux-surface kernel support. Debian may be a better long-term fit. Even rescued machines have opinions about distributions.

  13. Toshiba Portégé R930s The Toshiba Portégé R930s taught me Wi-Fi cracking basics and then refused, with impressive consistency, to update its BIOS. Even with the correct file available, the installer failed on every Windows version I tested. It had a 3G wireless card slot, a tiny reminder of a future that arrived and then quietly changed shape.

  14. 2013 15-inch MacBook Pro (Windows 11 experiment) The 2013 15-inch MacBook Pro became a Windows 11 experiment. I put Boot Camp and Windows 10 on it, then upgraded it to Windows 11 for a colleague. It has the same i7 3740QM quad-core as the E431, a familiar little engine crossing brands and operating systems.

  15. Cheap Acer clamshell laptop with 7th-gen i7 (Proxmox backup server) The cheap Acer clamshell with a 7th-gen i7 became a Proxmox backup server. Sometimes it bricks itself, then revives after sitting unpowered for a week like a dramatic Victorian patient. I had two HDDs stuffed into it; one replaced the DVD tray. There is dignity in storage, even when it rattles.

None of these computers were perfect. A few were barely sensible. But each one widened the map a little: games, Unix, school notes, development, Linux, storage, repair, rescue, failure, persistence. The machines came and went, or stayed too long in basements, or became servers after their screens gave out. Their resale value was beside the point.

They were worth something someday. Just not always in the currency anyone expected.
